<Intuition>
To measure the area of an oddly shaped field, you could slice it into narrow
strips, treat each strip as a straight-sided shape whose area you *can* compute,
and add them up. Narrower strips, closer answer.

That is **numerical integration**, and the trapezoidal rule is its simplest
form: each strip becomes a trapezoid, with its top being the straight line
between the curve's two endpoints.
</Intuition>

**Where you meet it.** Total energy consumed from a power reading taken every
second. Distance travelled from a speed log. The area under a
precision-recall curve. Expected value of anything described by a density you
can evaluate but not integrate on paper — which is most of them.

## The rule

<Api pkg="math-analysis" name="trapezoidalRule" /> splits `[lower, upper]` into
`intervals` equal panels, evaluates `f` at each boundary, and sums the
trapezoids.

Each panel is wrong by however much the real curve bulges away from the straight
line across it. That error shrinks as `O(h²)` in the panel width `h`, which
means something concrete and checkable: **double the number of panels and the
error should quarter.**

<Example id="math-analysis/trapezoidal-rule" />

## Where it stops improving

Push the panel count far enough and the measured error leaves the theoretical
line and flattens out. Nothing is broken — the *discretisation* error has fallen
below the *rounding* error accumulated across all those additions, and beyond
that point adding panels buys nothing but time.

That floor is why "just use more panels" is not a strategy. Getting past it
needs a better rule, not a finer one.
